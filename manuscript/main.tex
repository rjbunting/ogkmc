%\section{Introduction}

Kinetic modeling seeks to describe the reactivity and selectivity a catalyst has toward the conversion of reactants to products.
The desired outcome is to theoretically predict these key catalyst properties accurately to find new active and selective catalysts.
There are a variety of methods employed to achieve this.
The simplest method is from volcano plots, where the adsorption energies of key intermediates are calculated and fitted to a Bell–Evans–Polanyi principle relationship.
This can be combined with linear scaling, giving an optimal adsorption energy for a key intermediate to have the most activity toward a certain product.
With this, no explicit kinetic rate information of elementary reactions on the surface are included.
Beyond this method is micro-kinetic modeling, where the kinetic rates of key elementary steps on the surface are considered.
A steady state is assumed, where the gradient of the concentration of all surface species (the rate) equals 0.
Reactions are surface specific and not site specific, causing a reduction in information.
Despite this, surface species concentrations and the surface specific rates of elementary reactions can be discerned.
For atomic level information of surface reactions, kinetic Monte Carlo offers that resolution, where surface site occupation by species, and subsequent reactions between surface species are considered.
This allows the reaction to physically take place, going beyond surface concentrations.

Beyond the example of volcano plots, foreknowledge of the reaction mechanism is required, or, an exhaustive amount of reaction pathways are required to be considered to safely capture the dominant reaction pathway.
With this, if new mechanisms are the dominant pathway for new classes of catalysts, the reactivity would be underestimated.
A new method that builds the reaction network lazily, when new reactions on the surface become possible, would radically transform kinetic modeling.
Some methods, such as with adaptive KMC, have attempted to extend KMC as an on the fly method, yet no complete online method has been developed yet.
Here, a method is developed that considers possible graph evolutions as reaction classes.
With this method, denoted as online graph kinetic Monte Carlo (OGKMC), reaction products, reactivity, selectivity, and the dominant reaction pathway can be found, with only a catalyst structure, reactants, and reaction conditions as inputs.

OGKMC builds the catalyst and reactant structures as graphs.
The atoms are nodes and bonds are edges.
The nodes store the element, cartesian coordinates, classification, and an occupation flag.
Bonding is classified based on the overlapping elemental covalent radii between two atoms.
In this scenario, edges are built between these nodes.
As KMC should be performed from states that are local minima, this bonding is classified for structurally minimized structures.

The first step is building the catalyst structure and optimizing it with a potential.
This could be with classical or electronic structure based methods.
Bonding between atoms are set based on the covalent radii.
This produces the base graph with nodes and edges.
After this, the reactive part of the catalyst must be designated.
Designating a reactive part of the catalyst enables the complexity of new bonding being formed to be simplified.
This is also intuitive; subsurface atoms do not directly bond to reactants, with reactions taking place almost always at the surface.
In our implementation, any structure can be input, with support for both 2D surfaces and 3D catalysts; classification is by ray casting for 2D surfaces and through a convex hull for 3D catalysts.

With the surface defined, possible bonding points need to also be considered.
Bonding points on the surface are found based on all the geometrically possible coordinations the surface atoms, of covalent radius j + k, have with an adsorbate atom with covalent radius i.
Each element, with a unique covalent radius, will have different geometrically possible surface coordinations.
These possible coordinations are found by building edges between all nodes that fulfill the distance criteria of 2i + j + k.
With these edges built between all atoms, finding the possible sites is possible by finding all the cliques within the graph.
A 1-fold clique would be a site that coordinates with a single surface atom (top site); and a 3 or 4-fold clique would be a site that coordinates with three or four surface atoms (hollow site).
With efficient modern algorithms for finding cliques in a graph, all geometrically possible sites for an atom of a certain element can be found quickly with minimal compute.
This removes the need for a brute force sampling of the potential energy surface to find possible sites.
These geometrically possible sites are classed as anchors - named "anchors" as they are the fixed positions for finding adsorbate sites.

For adsorbates, they are first optimized with a potential and then structurally optimized.
Again, nodes and edges represent atoms and bonds as before, with edges classified with the same covalent radii as before.
In the code implementation, adsorbates are passed as smiles for ease of use.
Consider the case of the simplest adsorbate: a single atom.
This atom has all possible coordinations already defined.
From this, the site stability can be checked with a structure optimization to see if it is a minimum on the potential energy surface (as the KMC should go between minima states).
The final position is then checked for connectivity changes, i.e. bonds formed or broke during the optimization.
If the number of edges is not the same pre and post optimization, the site is unstable.
This allows sites to be pruned for an adsorbate, moving from geometrically possible to physically possible sites.
This allows the quantification of adsorption sites on the surface.

For multi-atom adsorbates, multiple anchors are considered together.
First, an array of all atom-atom distances in the adsorbate is made.
The possible coordinations an adorbate can have to the surface is based on anchor orbits.
To consider the flexibility of a molecule, a nominal buffer is applied to these distances to meet the criteria.
It considers, from an anchor of a designated atom, which other anchors match the criteria.
This gives an exhaustive list of geometrically possible adsorbate coordinations to the site.
With CO as an example in figXXXX, all possible site coordinations are considered (even those where the CO molecule coordinates to the surface through an oxygen atom!).
This removes any restrictions on the connectivity of the system.
Again, just like the single atom example, structural optimization is performed to find physically possible sites.
In practice, the vast majority of possible coordinations to the surface are pruned.

Reactions are considered to be graph transformations.
They can be given strict criteria for specific reactions.
The simplest example is the adsorption or, the reverse process, desorption.
Physically, it is a site going from unoccupied to occupied.
For our implementation, it is the same.
The graph transforms by changing the attribute of an adsorbate site node from unoccupied to occupied.


and classifying surface atoms.
These surface atoms are where reactions can take place.

This classification dictates which atoms can bond with reactants.

\section{Theory}
\subsection{What is a bond?}
\noindent
Bonding can be separated into two categories: ionic and covalent.
Ionic bonding can be explained simply from an electron transfer from one species to another, forming charged atoms.
These charged species are attracted to each other based on electrostatic attraction.
For covalent bonding, there is a quantum mechanical electron exchange energy, which can be more simply explained that the sharing of electrons forms a bond.
Deciding whether there is bonding between two atoms is difficult and depends on the definition of what is a bond.\cite{nbo,dori}
For us, we intuitively say a bond has formed if the covalent radii of two atoms overlap.
These covalent radii are dictated by the element of the atom.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=60ex]{figures/bond_formation.pdf}
    \caption{Defining a bond between atomic carbon and atomic oxygen}
    \label{fig:bond_definition}
\end{figure}

A simple example is given for carbon and oxygen (Fig.~\ref{fig:bond_definition})
The covalent radii are imported from ASE,\cite{ase} which are taken from ref.\cite{covradii}.
Regardless of whether the structure is stable, or if a quantum mechanical bond actually is formed, our strict definition is this.
With this definition of a bond, we can represent how molecules are connected to each other.

\subsection{Representing atoms as graphs}
Perhaps, as an imprinting of chemistry education, the graphical representation of molecules makes sense to chemists;
carbon can be bonded to 4 other atoms, whilst hydrogen can only be bonded to one.
Nodes are atomic nuclei, while edges are bonds.
Graphs only care about coordination however, and have no concern for position.
An example of this is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:molecular_graph}.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=60ex]{figures/molecular_graph.pdf}
    \caption{Example molecular structure and graph representation for benzene}
    \label{fig:molecular_graph}
\end{figure}

There is an information loss cost involved with this graphical representation.
Stereoisomerism and chirality are not considered (Fig.~\ref{fig:stereoisomers}).
However, what is lost is made up for by \textbf{rapid calculation}.
Comparing the similarity of structures is difficult and slow.
However, the comparison (or identification of a graph as an isomorph) can rapidly be deployed.\cite{cordella2004vf2,networkx}
This is important for us as it lets us \textbf{find if a surface has a certain graphical representation that can undergo a reaction}.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=60ex]{figures/stereoisomers.pdf}
    \caption{Example of a failure of graph representation, where isomorphic graphs are produced for cis/trans 1,2 dichloroethene}
    \label{fig:stereoisomers}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Graphical representation of surface chemistry}
\noindent

Just like graphs can describe molecules, they can also describe surface adsorbates.
However, surfaces are \textbf{much} bigger than molecules.
That raises an important question: How can we describe adsorbates on these very big surfaces that have many atoms/nodes?
We truncate the full graph into a subgraph with n shells (Fig.~\ref{fig:nshell}).

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=60ex]{figures/adsorbate_shell_expansion}
    \caption{Diagram of expanding to neighbours of neighbours of neighbours.
    Note, subsurface atoms are not shown.
    The package defaults to $n_{shells}=1$.
    This allows capturing different hollow sites in fcc metals and lateral interactions.
    }
    \label{fig:nshell}
\end{figure}

This allows us to describe transformations/chemical processes; a reaction happens and the graph representation changes.
The reactions are:
adsorption is an adsorption site becoming occupied;
diffusion is an occupied adsorption site becoming unoccupied and a neighbouring adsorption site becoming occupied;
bond formation is two occupied fragment adsorption sites becoming unoccupied and one product adsorption site becoming occupied.
The reverse of bond formation is bond dissociation.
The diffusion site representation is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:diffusion}.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=60ex]{figures/diffusion_site_process}
    \caption{Diagram showing the storage of diffusion reactions.
    The diffusion site is saved as a subgraph containing both hollow sites and their local surface environments.
    The example has $E_a=0.3$ eV and $\Delta E=0.1$ eV.}
    \label{fig:diffusion}
\end{figure}

\section{Method}
\subsection{Building a structure and classifying the surface sites}
\noindent
The reactive part of a catalyst is the surface.
For this, adsorbates are restricted to only bonding to the surface.
\textbf{Any} structure can be put in as an input, but the package has a basic input for slabs and nanoparticles.
The package classifies which atoms are surface atoms.
It does this with ray casting for periodic structures (slabs) and convex hull for 3D structures (nanoparticles).
These two surface classification routes are shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:surface_classification}.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=60ex]{figures/surface_classification_methods.pdf}
    \caption{Surface classification methods used before graph construction.
    Periodic slabs use ray casting, whilst nanoparticles use a convex hull.}
    \label{fig:surface_classification}
\end{figure}

The atoms are then converted into a graph.
Each atom becomes a node with an element, position, covalent radius, and type (surface or bulk).
Edges are added when covalent-radius neighbour-list cutoffs overlap.
The graph also stores the cell and periodic boundary information for loading structures in ASE for optimisation.

\subsection{Reactants}
\noindent
Reactants are supplied as SMILES strings for ease of input for the user.
The package uses RDKit to convert each SMILES string into a molecular graph.\cite{rdkit}
The molecule is relaxed with a calculator and a gas reference energy is found.

Reactants also define the chemical graph space for bond dissociation and association reactions.
For dissociation, every allowed bond in the molecular graph is cleaved once.
This creates pairs of fragment SMILES, which are canonicalised and deduplicated.
For bond formation, pairs of known species are joined at atoms with open valences.
Together, these operations produce reversible templates of the form $A + B \rightleftharpoons C$.
An ethane dissociation template produced this way is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:reactant_bond_templates}.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=60ex]{figures/reactant_bond_template_graph.pdf}
    \caption{Ethane dissociation as a molecular graph operation.
    Cleaving the C--C bond in \ce{CH3-CH3} gives two methyl fragment graphs, each represented as one carbon node bonded to three hydrogen nodes.
    These fragments define the dissociation side of the \ce{CH3 + CH3 <=> CH3-CH3} bond template.}
    \label{fig:reactant_bond_templates}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Per element possible sites}
\noindent
From the reactants, we can get the elements of the system.
For each element, a co-bonding graph is built on the surface atoms.
Two surface atoms are connected in this graph when an adsorbate atom of that element can geometrically bond to both of them.
Then, graph cliques can be made to give each distinct site on the graph, each with their own unique coordination.
Bigger elements will have bigger possible cliques.
This removes the need for having set positions for surface sites, as it is purely graph based.
These graphically unique sites on the surface are called \textbf{anchors}.
Once found, anchors are reused when molecular reactants are placed on the surface.
Example anchor-site classes are shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:anchor_sites}.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=60ex]{figures/anchor_site_selection.pdf}
    \caption{Anchor sites shown across a periodic surface unit cell.
    Only surface atoms and anchor positions are drawn; the legend identifies surface atoms, atop anchors, bridge anchors, hollow anchors, and the unit-cell boundary.
    The unit cell contains all translational copies of the unique anchor-site classes used to place adsorbates on the surface.}
    \label{fig:anchor_sites}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Adsorption sites}
\noindent
For single atoms, the adsorption site is trialed at the anchor site.
It then is optimised with a calculator, and its coordination (based on cutoff radii) is checked.
If the coordination stays the same, the site is stable and is accepted as a valid site.
If the coordination changes, it is rejected as the site is unstable.
This check (called \textbf{pruning}) is done to classify possible adsorbate sites versus geometrically possible adsorbate sites.
The graph is then updated across all unique cliques (multiple graphically identical cliques will be present across the graph).

Adsorption sites are built from a formed chain of anchor sites of each element.
For a chosen subset, the first anchor atom is placed on every distinct anchor point of the matching element.
The remaining anchor atoms are placed on anchor points whose distances match the gas-phase intramolecular distances within a tolerance.
For example, a \ce{CO} placement is only kept when the distance between the chosen C anchor and O anchor matches the gas-phase \ce{C-O} bond length within that tolerance.
This gives a candidate rigid placement of the molecule on the surface.
Candidates whose bonded surface cliques are not connected through the local surface graph are discarded.
The remaining placements are grouped by isomorphism of the union of their bonded surface environments.
This gives all geometrically possible coordinations for an adsorbate (Fig.~\ref{fig:adsorption_binding_cliques}).

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/adsorbate_binding_cliques.pdf}
    \caption{Possible \ce{CO} adsorption binding cliques are found from the gas-phase molecular bond distance, $r$.
    For multi-atom adsorbates, the array of all distances are used.
    This distance defines a tolerance window on the surface: after a C anchor is chosen, only O anchors at the matching separation are accepted.
    Each accepted C-O anchor pair stores the union of the C bonded and O bonded surface cliques as a candidate adsorption site.
    Note, there is the option for an atom (as long as it isn't the first anchor atom) to not be coordinated to the surface.}
    \label{fig:adsorption_binding_cliques}
\end{figure}

After enumeration, the representative geometry of each adsorption isomorphism class is optimised.
The optimisation first performs a calculator-free rigid-body refinement against the anchor points.
Then, when a calculator is available, the site is relaxed on the surface.
If the relaxation fails, or if the bonding graph changes so that the intended adsorbate is no longer the same species (breaks apart), the site is pruned.
Stable representative geometries are propagated to the other members of the same isomorphism class (identical sites on the surface).

\subsection{Diffusion sites}
\noindent
A diffusion site is a pair of adsorption-site members for the same adsorbate.
The pair is considered only if the two bonded surface-clique unions are local to each other.
For the stored graph representation, the package builds the surface-only ego graph around both endpoint sites and stamps the occupied endpoint labels into that graph.
Graph isomorphism then groups equivalent hops, so translating the same hop across the surface does not create a new reaction type.
Note, some pruning takes place as multiple hoppings between surface atom sharing sites is possible to the same unique site.
Take the FCC surface for example, a bridge site has 3 degenerate bridge sites it can hop to.
In this scenario, only one diffusion hop is taken for each unique adsorbate site pair.
The one with the smallest graph (least nodes and edges) is taken (this is the most localised/smallest distance hop between two degenerate sites).

\subsection{Bond sites}
\noindent
Bond sites are built from molecular graph templates.
For a coupling reaction, the template is $A + B \rightleftharpoons C$.
The package searches for a local triple of adsorption placements: one placement for fragment $A$, one for fragment $B$, and one for product $C$.
The $A$ and $B$ placements must be close enough on the surface to react, and the $C$ placement must be local to their combined surface environment.
$A$ and $B$ must share a surface atom, but cannot share the same coordination (cannot be ontop of each other).
The $C$ adsorbate site must share a surface atom to the $A$ + $B$ clique.
There is also an exception that if $C$ has no adsorbate sites (think van der Waals bonding like $CH_{4}$), it can react/desorb directly to/from the gas phase.

As with diffusion, the stored bond site is an isomorphism class, not a single surface position.
The graph contains the local environment of the two fragment placements and the product placement.
Equivalent triples are merged by graph isomorphism.
When KMC first encounters an applicable member of a new lateral class, the package relaxes the $A+B$ and $C$ endpoints.
If the coordination does not change, an NEB calculation for the bond-forming or bond-breaking path is completed for a barrier.\cite{henkelman2000cineb}

\subsection{Online expansion of bond sites}
\noindent
Bond chemistry can create species that were not present on the surface at the beginning of the simulation.
The package therefore expands the network after bond events fire.
When a coupling event forms a new product, adsorption and diffusion sites for that product are generated and the product can participate in later bond reactions.
When a dissociation event forms fragments, the fragment reaction channels are activated in the same way.
This maintains a small initial input while still allowing new surface species to appear during the trajectory.

\subsection{Lateral interactions}
\noindent
Lateral interactions are represented by changing the graph used to define a reaction rate (Fig.~\ref{fig:lateral}).
For a given site member, the package builds a local ego graph around the bonded surface clique and includes occupied neighbouring adsorbates as leaf nodes (despite not being within $n_{shells}$, the full adsorbate is capture).
Two members have the same lateral class only when these local occupied environments are graph-isomorphic.
There is fingerprinting to accelerate this (count nodes/elements/edges.)
The same adsorption, diffusion, or bond site can have different rates depending on which nearby adsorbates are present.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=60ex]{figures/lateral_interactions.pdf}
    \caption{Example of lateral interactions.}
    \label{fig:lateral}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Lazy calculation of processes}
\noindent
The expensive part of online KMC is not graph matching; it is relaxation and NEB.
Therefore, these are calculated lazily.
Enumeration creates possible adsorption, diffusion, and bond sites as graph objects.
During KMC, a site member is first checked for applicability.
The local lateral environment is then classified by graph isomorphism.
Only if that lateral class has not been seen before does the package run the corresponding stability calculation or NEB.
After a lateral class has been calculated, its energies and rates are stored, along with its graph.
All future members with the same graph environment reuse the stored rate.
For diffusion and bond reactions, the transition-state energy is used for both directions so that the forward and reverse rates remain thermodynamically consistent.

\subsection{The KMC part}
\noindent
KMC is run from the current graph state using the standard residence-time algorithm.\cite{bortz1975nfold,gillespie1977ssa}
At the start of a step, the package has a reaction list containing all currently possible adsorption, desorption, diffusion, bond-forming, and bond-breaking events.
Each entry stores the site member, direction, and rate.
For a calculated process, the rate is found from the barrier with the Eyring equation,\cite{eyring1935activated}
\begin{equation}
    k = \frac{k_{\mathrm{B}}T}{h}\exp\left(-\frac{E_a}{k_{\mathrm{B}}T}\right),
\end{equation}
where $E_a$ is the barrier, $T$ is the temperature, $k_{\mathrm{B}}$ is the Boltzmann constant, and $h$ is Planck's constant.

The total rate is the sum of all rates in the reaction list,
\begin{equation}
    R = \sum_{i=1}^{N} k_i ,
\end{equation}
where $k_i$ is the rate of reaction $i$ and $N$ is the number of reactions.
A random number $u_1$ between 0 and 1 is used to choose the KMC time step,
\begin{equation}
    \Delta t = -\frac{\ln u_1}{R}.
\end{equation}
This means that when the total rate is large, the simulation takes a smaller time step.
A second random number $u_2$ is used to choose which reaction fires.
The fired reaction, $\mu$, is the first reaction where the cumulative rate passes $u_2R$,
\begin{equation}
    \sum_{i=1}^{\mu-1} k_i < u_2R \leq \sum_{i=1}^{\mu} k_i .
\end{equation}
Therefore, the probability of reaction $i$ firing is
\begin{equation}
    P_i = \frac{k_i}{R}.
\end{equation}
A reaction with a larger rate occupies more of the cumulative rate interval, so it has a larger probability of firing.
When a reaction fires, the graph occupancy is updated.
Adsorption marks a site as occupied, desorption removes it, diffusion moves the occupied state from one site to another, and bond reactions replace the reactant adsorbates with the product adsorbate, or the reverse.

After firing, only the local reaction list is rebuilt.
The fired reaction changes a set of surface cliques.
Only sites that touch those cliques, or are within the lateral-interaction shell, need to be checked again.
The package removes stale reactions for those local sites, recomputes their applicability and rates, and inserts the new reactions back into the reaction list.
Reactions far away from the event are unchanged and keep their cached rates.
This greatly accelerates the building of the reaction list.
