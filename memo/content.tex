\section{Problem statement}
\subsection{Context}
\noindent
Catalysis contributes to \textbf{a third} of the global economy.
It dictates our lives through driving energy and food security, pharmaceutical synthesis, and manufacturing.
The theoretical exploration of new catalysts is required to further accelerate development in these key areas.
Theoretical exploration expedites catalyst discovery due to removing the need to experimentally synthesize and test candidates.

\subsection{Problem}
\noindent
Current kinetic models require the user to decide the important adsorbates, surface sites, reactions, and barriers before the simulation begins.
This is a severe limitation when the chemistry is not already known.
Even for a small catalytic network, manually deciding which diffusion, adsorption, desorption, and bond-forming events must be included can take longer than the KMC simulation itself.
The problem is therefore not only calculating a rate constant; it is deciding which rate constants should exist.
The goal of online KMC is to make those decisions automatically from the surface graph and the molecular graph.


\subsection{Significance}
\noindent
If the site and reaction network can be generated automatically, KMC can be used as a discovery tool instead of only a validation tool.
The user supplies the surface, gas-phase reactants, and a calculator.
The package then builds the adsorbate sites, finds local reaction motifs, and calculates barriers only when the simulation reaches the corresponding local environment.
This avoids precomputing an exhaustive reaction network, and makes it possible to explore systems where the dominant pathway is not known in advance.


\subsection{Goals}
\noindent
This project will be deemed a success when:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The online KMC model reproduces results of other KMC models.
    \item The product and dominant reaction pathway is found for a process, with only reactants and surface structure given as an input.
    \item Kinetic modeling is performed for a system that is unfeasible with current methods.
\end{itemize}

\section{Theory}
\subsection{What is a bond?}
\noindent
Bonding can be separated into two categories: ionic and covalent.
Ionic bonding can be explained simply from an electron transfer from one species to another, forming charged atoms.
These charged species are attracted to each other based on electrostatic attraction.
For covalent bonding, there is a quantum mechanical electron exchange energy, which can be more simply explained that the sharing of electrons forms a bond.
Deciding whether there is bonding between two atoms is difficult and depends on the definition of what is a bond.\cite{nbo,dori}
For us, we intuitively say a bond has formed if the covalent radii of two atoms overlap.
These covalent radii are dictated by the element of the atom.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=60ex]{figures/bond_formation.pdf}
    \caption{Defining a bond between atomic carbon and atomic oxygen}
    \label{fig:bond_definition}
\end{figure}

A simple example is given for carbon and oxygen (Fig.\ref{fig:bond_definition})
The covalent radii are imported from ASE, which are taken from ref.\cite{covradii}.
Regardless of whether the structure is stable, or if a quantum mechanical bond actually is formed, our strict definition is this.
With this definition of a bond, we can represent how molecules are connected to each other.

\subsection{Representing atoms as graphs}
Perhaps, as an imprinting of chemistry education, the graphical representation of molecules makes sense to chemists;
carbon can be bonded to 4 other atoms, whilst hydrogen can only be bonded to one.
Nodes are atomic nuclei, while edges are bonds.
Graphs only care about coordination however, and have no concern for position.
An example of this is shown in Fig.\ref{fig:molecular_graph}.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=60ex]{figures/molecular_graph.pdf}
    \caption{Example molecular structure and graph representation for benzene}
    \label{fig:molecular_graph}
\end{figure}

There is an information loss cost involved with this graphical representation.
Stereoisomerism and chirality are not considered (Fig.\ref{fig:stereoisomers}).
However, what is lost is made up for by \textbf{rapid calculation}.
Comparing the similarity of structures is difficult and slow.
However, the comparison (or identification of a graph as an isomorph) can rapidly be deployed.
This is important for us as it lets us \textbf{find if a surface has a certain graphical representation that can undergo a reaction}.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=60ex]{figures/stereoisomers.pdf}
    \caption{Example of a failure of graph representation, where isomorphic graphs are produced for cis/trans 1,2 dichloroethene}
    \label{fig:stereoisomers}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Graphical representation of surface chemistry}
\noindent

Just like graphs can describe molecules, they can also describe surface adsorbates.
However, surfaces are \textbf{much} bigger than molecules.
That raises an important question: How can we describe adsorbates on these very big surfaces that have many atoms/nodes?
We truncate the full graph into a subgraph with n shells.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=60ex]{figures/adsorbate_shell_expansion}
    \caption{Diagram of expanding to neighbours of neighbours of neighbours.
    Note, subsurface atoms are not shown.
    The package defaults to $n_{shells}=1$.
    This allows capturing different hollow sites in fcc metals and lateral interactions.
    }
    \label{fig:nshell}
\end{figure}

This allows us to describe transformations/chemical processes; a reaction happens and the graph representation changes.
The reactions are:
adsorption is an adsorption site becoming occupied;
diffusion is an occupied adsorption site becoming unoccupied and a neighbouring adsorption site becoming occupied;
bond formation is two occupied fragment adsorption sites becoming unoccupied and one product adsorption site becoming occupied.
The reverse of bond formation is bond dissociation.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=60ex]{figures/diffusion_site_process}
    \caption{Diagram showing the storage of diffusion reactions.
    The diffusion site is saved as a subgraph containing both hollow sites and their local surface environments.
    The example has $E_a=0.3$ eV and $\Delta E=0.1$ eV.}
    \label{fig:diffusion}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Building a structure and classifying the surface sites}
\noindent
The reactive part of a catalyst is the surface.
For this, adsorbates are restricted to only bonding to the surface.
\textbf{Any} structure can be put in as an input, but the package has a basic input for slabs and nanoparticles.
The package classifies which atoms are surface atoms.
It does this with ray casting for periodic structures (slabs) and convex hull for 3D structures (nanoparticles).

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=60ex]{figures/surface_classification_methods.pdf}
    \caption{Surface classification methods used before graph construction.
    Periodic slabs use ray casting, whilst nanoparticles use a convex hull.}
    \label{fig:surface_classification}
\end{figure}

The atoms are then converted into a graph.
Each atom becomes a node with an element, position, covalent radius, and type (surface or bulk).
Edges are added when covalent-radius neighbour-list cutoffs overlap.
The graph also stores the cell and periodic boundary information for loading structures in ASE for optimisation.

\subsection{Per element possible sites}
\noindent
For each element, a co-bonding graph is built on the surface atoms.
Two surface atoms are connected in this graph when an adsorbate atom of that element can geometrically bond to both of them.
Then, graph cliques can be made to give each distinct site on the graph, each with their own unique coordination.
Bigger elements will have bigger possible cliques.
This removes the need for having set positions for surface sites, as it is purely graph based.
These graphically unique sites on the surface are called \textbf{anchors}.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=60ex]{figures/anchor_site_selection.pdf}
    \caption{Anchor sites are generated from the maximum coordination allowed by the element-specific co-bonding graph.
    The maximum is found for each element separately.}
    \label{fig:anchor_sites}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Adsorption sites}
\noindent
For single atoms, the adsorption site is trialed at the anchor site.
It then is optimised with a calculator, and its coordination (based on cutoff radii) is checked.
If the coordination stays the same, the site is stable and is accepted as a valid site.
If the coordination changes, it is rejected as the site is unstable.
The graph is then updated across all unique cliques (multiple graphically identical cliques will be present across the graph).

###Show a coordination change of accepted and rejected cliques

Adsorption sites are built from a formed chain of anchor sites of each element.
For a chosen subset, the first anchor atom is placed on every distinct anchor point of the matching element.
The remaining anchor atoms are placed on anchor points whose distances match the gas-phase intramolecular distances within a tolerance.
This gives a candidate rigid placement of the molecule on the surface.
Candidates whose bonded surface cliques are not connected through the local surface graph are discarded.
The remaining placements are grouped by isomorphism of the union of their bonded surface environments.

After enumeration, the representative geometry of each adsorption isomorphism class is optimised.
The optimisation first performs a calculator-free rigid-body refinement against the anchor points.
Then, when a calculator is available, the site is relaxed on the surface.
If the relaxation fails, or if the bonding graph changes so that the intended adsorbate is no longer the same species, the site is pruned.
Stable representative geometries are propagated to the other members of the same isomorphism class.

    \caption{Adsorption sites are generated by choosing canonical molecular anchor-orbit subsets, placing them on surface anchor points, geometrically optimising, and pruning unstable relaxed geometries.}

\end{figure}

\subsection{Diffusion sites}
\noindent
A diffusion site is a pair of adsorption-site members for the same adsorbate.
The pair is considered only if the two bonded surface-clique unions are local to each other.
For the stored graph representation, the package builds the surface-only ego graph around both endpoint sites and stamps the occupied endpoint labels into that graph.
Graph isomorphism then groups equivalent hops, so translating the same hop across the surface does not create a new reaction type.

The barrier is not calculated when the diffusion pair is enumerated.
It is calculated when the KMC simulation first finds that one endpoint is occupied and the other endpoint is unoccupied.
At that point, the package relaxes the initial and final endpoints and runs a nudged elastic band calculation between them.
The NEB saddle gives the transition-state energy, from which the forward and reverse diffusion rates are calculated using the Eyring form.
The schematic in Fig.\ref{fig:diffusion} shows the stored two-site process.

\subsection{Bond sites}
\noindent
Bond sites are built from molecular graph templates.
For a coupling reaction, the template is $A + B \rightleftharpoons C$.
The package searches for a local triple of adsorption placements: one placement for fragment $A$, one for fragment $B$, and one for product $C$.
The $A$ and $B$ placements must be close enough on the surface to react, and the $C$ placement must be local to their combined surface environment.
Degenerate cases where two species claim the same adsorption clique are discarded.

As with diffusion, the stored bond site is an isomorphism class, not a single surface position.
The graph contains the local environment of the two fragment placements and the product placement.
Equivalent triples are merged by graph isomorphism.
When KMC first encounters an applicable member of a new lateral class, the package relaxes the $A+B$ and $C$ endpoints and runs an NEB calculation for the bond-forming or bond-breaking path.

\subsection{Online expansion of bond sites}
\noindent
Bond chemistry can create species that were not present on the surface at the beginning of the simulation.
The package therefore expands the network after bond events.
When a coupling event forms a new product, adsorption and diffusion sites for that product are generated and the product can participate in later bond reactions.
When a dissociation event forms fragments, the fragment reaction channels are activated in the same way.
This keeps the initial input small: the user can provide the gas-phase reactants and the surface, while new surface-only species are introduced as the trajectory reaches them.

\subsection{Lazy calculation of barriers}
\noindent
The expensive part of online KMC is not graph matching; it is relaxation and NEB.
Therefore barriers are calculated lazily.
Enumeration creates possible adsorption, diffusion, and bond sites as graph objects.
During KMC, a site member is first checked for applicability.
The local lateral environment is then classified by graph isomorphism.
Only if that lateral class has not been seen before does the package run the corresponding stability calculation or NEB.

After a lateral class has been calculated, its energies and rates are cached.
All future members with the same graph environment reuse the stored rate.
For diffusion and bond reactions, the transition-state energy is used for both directions so that the forward and reverse rates remain thermodynamically consistent.
The rate is then calculated with an Eyring expression,
$k = \kappa k_{\mathrm{B}}T h^{-1}\exp(-E_a/k_{\mathrm{B}}T)$.
Adsorption rates additionally include the gas partial pressure.

\subsection{Lateral interactions}
\noindent
Lateral interactions are represented by changing the graph used to define a reaction rate.
For a given site member, the package builds a local ego graph around the bonded surface clique and includes occupied neighbouring adsorbates as leaf nodes.
Two members have the same lateral class only when these local occupied environments are graph-isomorphic.
Thus the same adsorption, diffusion, or bond site can have different rates depending on which nearby adsorbates are present.

This gives a practical compromise between a lattice model with fixed pair interactions and a fully new calculation for every surface configuration.
The KMC loop only recomputes sites in the surface shell affected by the last event.
If a new occupied environment appears, the corresponding barrier is calculated and cached.
If the environment has appeared before, the cached rate is reused.
